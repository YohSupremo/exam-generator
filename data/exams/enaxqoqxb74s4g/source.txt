DATA ANALYSIS  
 
DATA ANALYSIS  
●​
It means the intelligence generated from 
reviewing audit-related information, often 
using technology. Like other types of data 
analytics, audit analytics typically involve 
analyzing large sets of numbers to find 
actionable audit insights.  
 
C1 
THE FIVE STEPS TO GET YOU STARTED 
WITH DATA ANALYSIS 
 
STEP 1: DEFINE QUESTIONS AND GOALS 
●​
The first step in data analysis is to clearly 
define 
your 
questions 
and 
goals. 
Like 
creating a hypothesis before an experiment, 
you should be asking a targeted question 
before searching the data for an answer. For 
example, 
questions 
about 
overall 
performance can be open-ended and it can 
be hard to pinpoint which metrics are needed 
for analysis. Instead, it is more advantageous 
to ask questions such as "How have certain 
metrics changed over time?" and "Do these 
metrics correlate with others, and if so, 
how strongly?" These types of questions 
have 
a specific focus which will help 
determine the type of analysis needed and 
what data is the most relevant to include.  
 
STEP 2: COLLECT DATA 
●​
Before you can start analyzing, there needs to 
be data available for use. Data can include 
sales records, customer demographics, lead 
tracking, net promoter scores, and more. 
When using a business intelligence tool, it is 
important to make sure that all of the data is 
accessible and the proper connections are 
set between your data warehouse and your 
BI tool of choice. Ultimately the volume of 
data required will depend on the question 
you wish to answer. Though you may only 
need a subset of the data collected, not 
having enough data can skew the results of 
your analysis. 
 
STEP 3: DATA WRANGLING 
●​
Now that you have all of your data in one 
place, it is important to clean the data before 
beginning the analysis portion of this 
process. A large part of the cleansing process 
includes making sure that the data is in a 
usable format. This entails searching for 
outliers, dealing with null values, and looking 
for data that may have been incorrectly 
input. Often this can be a lengthy and 
arduous process.  
 
STEP 4: DETERMINE ANALYSIS 
●​
Once the relevant data is available and 
cleansed, it is time to analyze. Choosing a 
method of analysis will heavily depend on 
the question or goals defined earlier and the 
type of analysis needed. 
●​
Diagnostic analysis uses data to search for 
the cause of, and a solution to, an existing 
problem. 
●​
Descriptive analysis is a way to describe the 
data by summarizing key sections. 
●​
Predictive analysis combines historical data 
and statistical modeling to forecast how 
certain metrics will perform in the future. 
 
STEP 5: INTERPRET RESULTS 
●​
After analyzing your data, it is important to 
interpret the results. Simply put, what are 
you learning from the results of your 
analysis? Combining multiple visualizations 
in one report enables the users to tell a story 
about their data that leaders can use to take 
actions, make changes, or refocus efforts 
accordingly. This step is key to gaining 
insights from the data and being able to 
apply these insights to your business.  
●​
With these steps, you will be able to use data 
analysis to unlock powerful new insights 
that can be turned into actionable results. 
These insights could lead to more questions 
that prompt you to dig even deeper into the 
data and start the analysis cycle again.

1. SIGNATURE-BASED ANALYSIS  
●​
Also 
known 
as 
Misuse 
Detection, 
this 
method scans network packets and system 
logs for specific patterns, byte sequences, or 
malicious 
instruction 
code—known 
as 
"signatures"—that match recorded threats.  
●​
Pros: Highly accurate for known threats; 
generates very few false positives.  
●​
Cons: 
Entirely 
blind 
to 
new, 
zero-day 
vulnerabilities and morphing malware.  
 
2. ANOMALY-BASED ANALYSIS  
●​
This method relies on machine learning or 
statistical modeling to build a rigid baseline 
profile 
of 
standard 
system 
or 
network 
operations. Once established, the system logs 
any 
deviation from this baseline as a 
potential attack.  
●​
Pros: Capable of detecting brandnew, novel, 
or zeroday threats.  
●​
Cons: High rate of false positives caused by 
legitimate, unusual user spikes or system 
changes.  
 
3. STATEFUL PROTOCOL ANALYSIS  
●​
Also known as Specification-Based analysis, 
this method compares network activity 
against universal vendor profiles of how 
protocols should structurally behave. It tracks 
the state of network pairings over time. This 
is different from anomaly detection which 
learns "normal" behavior from history. 
●​
Pros: Easily catches protocol manipulation, 
illegal commands, and scanning attempts.  
●​
Cons: Consumes heavy system resource 
overhead 
to 
continuously 
track 
state 
conditions across layers.

The Presentation of Findings is the pivotal

lifecycle where auditors formally deliver, 
explain, and contextualize verified audit 
results to management, process owners, and 
governance committees. Rather than simply 
handing over raw data, this process translates

○​
Core Purpose: Transforms complex

into 
actionable 
business insights. 
○​
Strategic Role: Connects technical 
analysis 
with

decision-making and remediation. 
 
DESCRIPTION 
●​
This activity typically takes place in a formal 
closing meeting, executive presentation, or

TWO APPROACHES TO RISK ASSESSMENT 
●​
Qualitative Risk Assessment 
○​
Uses descriptive, judgment-based 
ratings like Low/Medium/High rather 
than hard numbers. It's fast and easy 
to communicate, relying on expert 
experience and team discussion. 
●​
Quantitative Risk Assessment 
○​
Uses numerical, data-driven values

precise

●​
Example 
○​
A startup gives a new vendor a quick 
"Medium" rating in a team discussion 
(qualitative), while a bank calculates a 
$2.3M expected annual loss before 
approving a large loan (quantitative). 
 
QUALITATIVE RISK ASSESSMENT 
●​
Uses descriptive scales (high, medium, low) 
●​
Relies on expert judgment and experience 
●​
Requires minimal mathematical calculations 
●​
Provides quick assessment results 
●​
Works well with limited historical data 
 
 
QUANTITATIVE RISK ASSESSMENT 
●​
Uses numerical values and probabilities 
●​
Requires statistical analysis tools 
●​
Demands detailed historical data 
●​
Produces specific numerical outcomes 
●​
Takes longer to complete

●​

○​
The 
sustained 
delivery 
of 
an 
organization's critical services and 
functions throughout a disruption,

MAXIMUM TOLERABLE DOWNTIME 
 
MAXIMUM TOLERABLE DOWNTIME 
●​
also called Maximum Tolerable Period of 
Disruption (MTPD) — is the longest period a 
critical 
business 
function 
can 
be 
unavailable 
before 
the 
consequences 
become 
unacceptable, 
and 
potentially 
threaten the survival of the organization itself.  
●​
It is not a target to aim for — it is the outer 
boundary every recovery strategy must stay 
inside  
 
 
●​
Why it matters?  
○​
Anchors every other recovery metric 
(RTO, RPO, WRT)  
○​
Differs 
by 
function 
— 
payroll 
tolerates 
more 
downtime 
than 
trading  
○​
Drives investment priorities and 
recovery strategy design  
 
WHAT DRIVES MTD? 
●​
Financial Impact  
○​
Lost revenue, contractual penalties, 
and 
cash-flow 
strain 
that 
accumulate the longer a function 
stays down  
●​
Reputational Damage  
○​
Erosion of customer, investor, and 
media trust — damage that often 
outlasts the outage itself  
●​
Regulatory & Legal Obligations  
○​
Compliance deadlines and statutory 
duties that create hard limits on 
acceptable disruption  
●​
Operational Dependencies  
○​
Downstream 
processes 
and 
contractual SLAs with partners that 
fail once this function is unavailable 
too long  
 
THE COST OF DOWNTIME ESCALATES OVER TIME 
●​
Impact rarely rises in a straight line — it 
accelerates. Early hours may be absorbed by 
buffers and goodwill; each additional day 
compounds financial, legal, and reputational 
cost until it approaches the MTD ceiling  
●​
MTD marks the point beyond which the 
curve 
becomes 
unacceptable 
— 
by 
definition, not by degree  
 
HOW RTO, RPO & WRT FIT WITHIN MTD 
 
●​
RPO looks backward from the disruption to 
set how much data loss is tolerable. RTO 
measures the recovery effort itself. WRT 
covers 
verification 
before 
normal 
work 
resumes. Together, RPO + RTO + WRT must fit 
comfortably inside the MTD boundary

Consult Stakeholders & Regulators  
-​
Gather input from process owners, 
customers, and compliance bodies

WHY REVIEW THE PLAN?  
●​
A continuity plan is only accurate the day it 
is written. Organizations change, staff turn 
over, systems evolve, and new threats 
emerge — without regular review, the plan 
quietly drifts out of step with reality  
●​
PEOPLE CHANGE - Roles, contacts, and 
responsibilities move on 
●​
SYSTEMS CHANGE - New platforms replace 
what describes  
●​
RISK CHANGE  - New threats emerge that 
earlier plans never considered  
 
WHEN TO TRIGGER A REVIEW  
●​
SCHEDULED INTERVAL  
○​
A baseline review, typically annual, 
regardless of whether anything else 
has changed  
●​
ORGANIZATIONAL 
OR 
TECHNOLOGY 
CHANGE  
○​
Restructures, 
mergers, 
new 
systems, or office moves that alter 
dependencies  
●​
AFTER AN INCIDENT OR AUDIT FINDING  
○​
Real 
events 
and 
independent 
findings 
are 
the 
most 
reliable 
evidence a plan needs updating

Validate Contacts & Dependencies

current

TYPES OF BACKUPS 
 
BACKUP 
●​
Backing up data means creating copies of 
files and systems so they can be restored if 
the originals are lost, damaged, or corrupted. 
There are several common types of backups, 
each 
with 
different 
tradeoffs in speed, 
storage 
space, 
and 
recovery 
time. 
Understanding 
these 
types 
helps 
organizations choose the right strategy for 
protecting their data. 
 
1. FULL BACKUP 
●​
slowest to create, fastest to restore, uses the 
most storage  
 
2. INCREMENTAL BACKUP 
●​
An incremental backup only copies data that 
has changed since the last backup, whether 
that was a full backup or another incremental 
backup. This makes it much faster and more 
storage-efficient than a full backup, but 
restoring 
data 
can 
take 
longer 
since 
multiple backup sets are needed.  
 
3. DIFFERENTIAL BACKUP 
●​
A differential backup copies all data that has 
changed since the last full backup, not since 
the last backup of any kind. Over time, 
differential 
backups 
grow 
larger 
than 
incremental ones, but they are faster to 
restore because only two backup sets are 
needed: the full backup and the latest 
differential.  
 
4. MIRROR BACKUP 
●​
A mirror backup creates an exact, real-time 
copy of the source data, similar to a full 
backup but continuously updated. Unlike 
other backup types, a mirror backup does not 
keep older versions of files. If a file is deleted 
from the source, it is also removed from the 
mirror, which can be risky if data loss goes 
unnoticed.

Full backup

slowest to create, fastest to restore, 
uses the most storage

POST INCIDENT REVIEW 
 
POST INCIDENT REVIEW 
●​
What happened, and when 
●​
Were the documented procedures followed, 
and were they adequate? 
●​
What slowed the recovery down? 
●​
What do we do  differently next time? 
 
FIND THE REAL CAUSE, NOT JUST THE SYMPTOM 
●​
Symptom: "The server went down."​
Real cause: nobody was watching the disk 
space, and nobody had ever tested a restore.​
An easy method: keep asking "why?" until 
you reach something you can actually fix. This 
is called the 5 Whys. 
●​
Check if the backup really worked 
This is where the restore gets judged. Two 
questions: 
○​
How much data did we lose, and 
could we afford to lose that much? 
(This is the RPO.) 
○​
How long were we down, and was 
that fast enough? (This is the RTO.) 
○​
A backup that turns out damaged or 
three weeks old is itself a finding. 
●​
Every finding needs a person in charge and 
a due date.  
○​
Then update the response plan, the 
backup policy, and the step-by-step 
guides. 
○​
Numbers to track: how long it took 
to notice the problem (MTTD), how 
long it took to fix it (MTTR), total 
downtime, data lost, and cost. 
 
F 
DOCUMENTATION 
 
DOCUMENTATION 
●​
Documentation 
starts the moment you 
detect the incident  
●​
You do not write it at the end.  
○​
It is a running log. Responders write 
down each step, with the time, while 
they are still working on the problem.  
 
WHAT TO WRITE DOWN: 
●​
Incident ID: one number that connects all 
the records together 
●​
Discovery: when it was found, how it was 
found, and who found it 
●​
Scope: which systems, applications, and data 
were affected 
●​
Severity: how serious it was rated, and who 
rated it 
●​
Actions: every step taken, in order, with the 
exact time 
●​
Notifications: who was told, when, and 
through what channel 
●​
Evidence: copies of logs, file checksums, and 
a record of who handled them 
●​
Backup used: which backup was restored, its 
date, and whether it was tested 
●​
Write facts only. Never write guesses. 
○​
The log is evidence. It can be used in 
court, in an insurance claim, or in a 
government 
audit. 
Keep 
access 
restricted, and keep the log for as 
long as your policy requires.

REQUIRED BY LAW 
●​
Republic Act 10173, the Data Privacy Act of 
2012. NPC Circular 16-03.  
○​
72 hours: notify the National Privacy 
Commission and the people whose

when you know, or have good reason 
to believe, that a breach happened. It 
does 
not

○​
5 days: submit the full breach report,

●​
The notice must state what happened, what

This does get enforced. H&M Philippines was
